Best Digio Alternatives for DPDP-Compliant Aadhaar KYC (2026)

Digio's Aadhaar eSign and KYC line predates DPDP consent rules, sold separately via its CoTrust add-on. Compare 7 real Digio alternatives, including Hypersign.

Hypersign Compliance Team·August 28, 2026·8 min read

Digio has run India's Aadhaar eSign and KYC infrastructure since 2016, CERT-In audited and compliant as per CCA and UIDAI guidelines, with 2,000+ enterprises and 100 million+ individual users on its own about page. Its DigiKYC and DigiSign products cover Aadhaar OTP eSign, document OCR, liveness, video KYC, and CKYCRR 2.0 automation, real, mature infrastructure. Neither product page mentions DPDP anywhere on it.

That's not because Digio ignores India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act. It sells a separate product, CoTrust, built specifically for DPDPA consent and privacy compliance. The gap is that CoTrust sits apart from DigiKYC and DigiSign as a second product a team has to evaluate and integrate on its own, not something built into the Aadhaar eSign or KYC flow itself. Below are seven real Digio alternatives, sourced from each vendor's own site, including the one that builds DPDP-aligned consent into the verification flow rather than selling it as an add-on.

What to Look for in a Digio Alternative

Before comparing vendors, it helps to know what actually separates a good fit from a bad one:

  • DPDP built into the flow, not sold beside it. A separate consent-manager product still means integrating two systems and reconciling two audit trails.
  • Aadhaar eSign maturity. CERT-In audit status and CCA/UIDAI-guideline compliance aren't optional for a legally binding signature in India.
  • Published pricing versus a sales quote. Whether a vendor puts a rate card on its site is a decent proxy for how easy it is to start with as a smaller team.
  • Reuse that isn't network-locked. A verified identity should be reusable across compliant partners, not just other customers of the same vendor.
  • Data minimization as a structural property. Selective disclosure that returns a pass/fail result is a different architecture than storing a full document and restricting access to it later.

Where Digio Actually Fits

Digio's Aadhaar eSign is genuinely strong: CERT-In audited, compliant as per CCA and UIDAI guidelines, with backend key-pair generation and a DSC issued by a Certifying Authority that Digio describes as equivalent to Class-II DSC, backed by multiple CA-ESP integrations for failover. DigiKYC adds ID OCR, DigiLocker integration, 20+ liveness checks, selfie face-match with geo-tagging, video KYC, and CKYCRR 2.0 automation through DigiCKYC, a wide product suite spanning eSign, KYC/KYB, eNACH, and Account Aggregator connectivity under one company.

On DPDP specifically: Digio built CoTrust for exactly this, ML-based data discovery and classification, purpose-granular consent tied to Records of Processing Activities, Data Subject Request handling, and a breach-notification workflow referencing DPDP's 72-hour window in Digio's own blog. But CoTrust is its own product page, its own integration, and its own evaluation cycle, separate from DigiKYC and DigiSign. Neither of those two product pages mentions DPDP, purpose limitation, or consent management at all, verified directly against both pages. A team adopting Digio for Aadhaar eSign and KYC today gets a mature, audited signing and verification stack, and a second sales conversation if it also wants DPDP-structured consent built around that same data.

Pricing isn't published on either DigiKYC or DigiSign's own pages either, every plan routes through a sales conversation. None of that makes Digio a bad product, its Aadhaar eSign legal validity and decade of enterprise volume are real. It's just a different shape of solution than a platform where consent, purpose-binding, and erasure ship inside the same verification call.

1. Hypersign

Hypersign is the one name on this list where DPDP-aligned consent is structural to the KYC flow itself, not a second product bolted on beside it. Document OCR across 189+ countries, biometric liveness and face match, a decision engine, and consent management all ship on the Starter plan, no separate consent-manager integration required.

The specific mechanics: consent is captured per purpose, identity verification, AML screening, document storage, marketing, each independently revocable, versioned, timestamped, and cryptographically signed, with consent receipts issued as W3C Verifiable Credentials. Selective disclosure via zero-knowledge proofs and BBS+ signatures returns a pass/fail or threshold result instead of the underlying document, so data minimization is a property of the check itself rather than a policy layered on afterward. A right-to-erasure engine purges and anonymizes records on request, fires a webhook, and issues a signed erasure certificate, well inside DPDP's 30-day statutory response window.

Every verified identity is issued as a W3C DID and Verifiable Credential, reusable with any compliant partner instead of staying locked inside one vendor's database, and Aadhaar verification is handled the way UIDAI requires it: masked to the last four digits on return, processed and stored within India. Pricing is $500 per quarter for the first year (about 100 checks per quarter included), then $0.75 per verification flat with no minimum from year two. For a team comparing digio vs hypersign specifically on the DPDP question, that's the direct trade: Digio's CoTrust as a second product to integrate, against consent, minimization, and erasure built into the same API that runs the Aadhaar check.

2. Leegality

Leegality is a direct Digio competitor on eSign and eStamping, with real published pricing: the Basic plan runs Aadhaar eSign at ₹25 per signatory, non-Aadhaar eSign at ₹15 per signatory, and digital stamping at ₹45 per stamp paper plus stamp duty and a ₹500 per-order fee, with a minimum of 100 eSign credits or 30 stamp papers. Premium, Scale, and Enterprise tiers move to custom, demo-gated pricing.

Leegality follows the same pattern as Digio on DPDP: it markets ConsentIn, described on its own site as "India's first DPDP Act compliant Consent Manager," as a separate product from its core eSign line rather than something built into the signing flow itself.

3. Signzy

Signzy runs KYC, KYB, AML, and eSign under one platform, with more than 1,800 financial institutions and technology companies as customers per its own about page, ISO 27001, SOC 2 Type II, and PCI-DSS certification, and offices across six locations. Unlike Digio, Leegality, or IDfy, Signzy states its DPDP position directly on its own about page: it audits its systems against GDPR and India's DPDP Act and offers regional data-residence options, including India, to satisfy RBI-style localization requirements.

That's a real, on-page DPDP claim rather than a separate product. What Signzy doesn't publish is a reusable, cross-vendor credential model or a rate card, pricing routes through a sales quote.

4. IDfy

IDfy runs a KYC, onboarding, and fraud-detection platform, and like Digio and Leegality, addresses DPDP through a separate product line: Privy, marketed as "India's First Full-Stack DPDP Compliance & Privacy Governance Platform," covering consent management, Data Principal Rights handling, PII discovery, incident and breach management with a 72-hour response process, third-party risk, and DPO dashboards.

Privy is explicitly built on top of IDfy's KYC data expertise but sold and evaluated as its own platform, the same bolt-on shape as Digio's CoTrust and Leegality's ConsentIn. Neither IDfy's core KYC APIs nor Privy publish pricing, both route through a demo request.

5. Setu

Setu, part of Pine Labs, runs an API-first Aadhaar eSign and eKYC infrastructure with smart routing between NSDL and eMudhra to improve success rates, support for up to six signers per document, and funnel analytics. Pricing is public and simple: ₹12 per successful verification for up to 1,000 monthly verifications, with bulk pricing available above that through sales.

Setu's eSign page carries no DPDP Act mention at all, only a generic "fully data privacy compliant" line and an Aadhaar-specific consent notice limiting use to signing, per UIDAI and NSDL e-Gov guidelines. It's a clean infrastructure API, not a compliance platform.

6. Zoop.one

Zoop.one offers an Aadhaar eSign and eKYC API with add-ons for face verification, liveness detection, OCR, and dynamic data retrieval from a name and phone number. Pricing isn't disclosed on the product page.

Zoop's Aadhaar eSign API page carries no DPDP Act mention anywhere, verified directly against the page, only generic language about complying with data privacy and security regulations. Of every alternative here, it's the one with the least DPDP-specific positioning of any kind, no bolt-on product, no on-page claim.

7. HyperVerge

HyperVerge is the most India-focused name on this list outside Hypersign, built around passive liveness and DeepfakeSafe detection certified to ISO 30107-3 Level 2, claiming 500+ enterprise customers and 2 billion-plus verifications, and marketing "100% RBI-compliant onboarding" for NBFCs and banks.

That RBI claim is a banking-license onboarding claim, not a DPDP data-protection claim, and no DPDP-specific language appears anywhere on HyperVerge's site. Pricing isn't published, every plan routes to a demo request.

Choosing the Right Digio Alternative

If Aadhaar eSign legal maturity and a decade of audited enterprise volume matter most, Digio's own DigiSign, or Leegality's transparently priced eSign and eStamp line, are both solid choices. If an on-page DPDP claim backed by data-residence options matters more than a reusable credential model, Signzy fits. If the lowest published per-check price for Aadhaar infrastructure is the priority, Setu's ₹12-per-verification rate is hard to beat.

But if the goal is DPDP-aligned consent, purpose-binding, selective disclosure, and erasure built into the same verification call rather than a second product to integrate, that's the specific gap none of the other six digio alternatives here close, CoTrust, ConsentIn, and Privy included, since all three exist beside the KYC or eSign flow rather than inside it.
