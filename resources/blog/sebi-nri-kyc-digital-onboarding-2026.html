SEBI NRI KYC Rules 2026: Digital KYC Checklist for Brokers, AMCs and DPs

From 3 December 2026, SEBI lets intermediaries complete NRI KYC digitally for clients in FATF-compliant countries. The circular's exact steps, the ten video IPV controls, the KRA upload deadline, and what to build before day one.

Hypersign Compliance Team·November 19, 2026·7 min read

SEBI's circular of 4 November 2026 removes the rule that tied NRI KYC to Indian soil. Under the existing KYC Master Circular, an intermediary had to ensure the client sat physically in India during digital KYC, which pushed every NRI, OCI cardholder and foreign national abroad back onto paper forms, attested copies and international courier. The circular, titled "Review of Know Your Client process for individual Person Resident Outside India clients", relaxes that requirement from 3 December 2026 for clients located in a FATF-compliant country.

Most coverage so far speaks to investors. The harder questions sit with the brokers, AMCs, depository participants and AIF managers who have to build the flow, because the circular switches nothing on by default: paragraph 4.1 says an intermediary "may" accept digital submission. Firms that wire the KRA and CKYC lookups, the document routes and the ten video IPV controls correctly before 3 December get an onboarding channel some competitors will not have on day one, and paragraph 5.6.3 leaves ultimate responsibility for every record with the intermediary, whichever vendor runs the flow.

What the SEBI NRI KYC Circular Changes, and What It Leaves Alone

Paragraph 3 defines an individual PROI client as an NRI, an OCI cardholder or a foreign national. The same process applies to re KYC (para 5.10), and the circular supersedes SEBI's December 2025 relaxation on geo-tagging for NRI re KYC outright (para 8.3).

"FATF compliant" follows the details FATF itself publishes (footnote 5), so the country list moves whenever FATF updates its high-risk and monitored jurisdictions. Hard-coding it into an onboarding rule set is a maintenance liability.

The NRI KYC Flow Under Paragraph 5, Step by Step

Paragraph 5 starts with lookup, not collection. The intermediary asks for the client's PAN and CKYC ID, retrieves the existing record from the KYC Registration Agency (KRA) and the Central KYC Records Registry (CKYCRR), and collects fresh details only when something changed, the record is incomplete, an officially valid document (OVD) has lapsed, or enhanced due diligence calls for it (paras 5.2 and 5.3).

Two operational details matter. If an intermediary cannot verify an Indian or international mobile number, every communication goes to the verified email (para 5.5.7). Every document must be in English or carry an attested translation (para 5.9), so a local-language residence permit needs catching at upload, not at KRA validation.

KYC Documents for NRIs, and Where "Fully Online" Breaks

The KYC documents for NRIs are a short list: PAN (verified, not collected), a passport copy, the OCI card where applicable, one OVD for identity and overseas address, and the KYC form on the CERSAI individual template under an electronic signature as the Information Technology Act defines it (paras 5.5.3 and 5.5.5). A foreign national whose OVD carries no address can submit a foreign government document with a letter from their embassy or mission in India. Three provisions, though, keep a remote journey from being fully digital by default.

The fully remote path runs through paragraph 5.5.6(b): DigiLocker, an issuing-authority e-document through a verifiable mechanism, OTP, biometric or face-based Aadhaar e-KYC, or a copy certified by a notary, an Indian embassy or consulate, or an eligible bank official. Aadhaar OTP goes to the mobile number UIDAI holds, so for an NRI whose Indian SIM has lapsed, face authentication is the route to design for.

The Ten Video IPV Controls, and Who Owns Each One

Paragraph 5.5.8(b) sets ten conditions for video in-person verification (VIPV), and paragraph 4.4 repeats two of them for emphasis: an authorised official on every call and concurrent audit of the process. For any intermediary buying rather than building its IPV verification layer, the list doubles as the vendor evaluation checklist.

Paragraph 5.5.8(c) lets an intermediary add controls under its own risk policy, and paragraph 5.8 requires the whole KYC infrastructure to comply with SEBI's Cyber Security and Cyber Resilience Framework (CSCRF). That puts a vendor's hosting and data flows inside the intermediary's CSCRF scope, so ask for that evidence during procurement.

Build, Buy, or Wait: Where This Leaves an Intermediary

Because the circular permits rather than requires the digital route, the first decision is commercial: whether NRI and foreign-national volume justifies a remote channel by 3 December or a quarter later. For firms that decide yes, the options split three ways.

  • Already running agent-assisted video KYC. HyperVerge and Signzy both sell dedicated video KYC products, and Signzy pairs it with CKYCR integration built over years of serving Indian banks. Extending an existing stack with the FATF-country IP rule and the wet-signature step is the shortest path.
  • No video layer yet. Buy the verification stack (country check, PAN, passport, Aadhaar, liveness, face match, screening and the video IPV call) from one vendor, and keep the authorised official, the auditor and the KRA upload in-house, where paragraph 5.6.3 places ultimate responsibility anyway.
  • A small NRI book. Waiting is defensible. Paragraph 4.1 permits the new route without mandating it.
